Phone photos vs. hiring a photographer for your restaurant

You don't need a photographer for everything. Phones are good now. But there are a few places where it's worth paying for photos, and it's good to know which is which.

When phone photos are fine

For day-to-day social posts, your phone is great. Instagram stories, behind-the-scenes, today's special on the board, the new keg going on. These are quick, they're meant to feel casual, and nobody expects them to be perfect.

Honestly, a lot of that stuff looks better when it's a little rough. It feels real. Keep doing it.

When to hire a restaurant photographer

The photos that stick around, and that people use to decide whether to come in, are a different story. That's where I'd spend the money:

  • Your menu. Printed, digital or QR, the photos need to be consistent and clean.
  • Your website. It's often the first real look people get at your place.
  • Delivery apps. Every dish, all matching, all readable at thumbnail size.
  • Openings and relaunches. You get one chance to introduce the place.
  • Press. Writers and editors want sharp, well-lit photos they can run.

These photos get looked at over and over, by a lot of people, for a long time. It's worth getting them right once. That's what a menu shoot is for.

How to take better food photos with your phone

For everything else, a few simple things make a big difference.

Use window light

Take the plate to a window. Daylight is the easiest way to make food look good. Put the plate near the window with the light coming from the side or slightly behind it.

Turn off the overhead lights

Restaurant lighting is great for dinner and bad for photos. Warm overhead bulbs make everything look yellow and flat. If you're using window light, turn off the lights above the table so the colors don't mix.

Clean the plate

Wipe the rim. Drips and smudges you'd never notice at the table look huge in a photo. Take a second with a clean towel before you shoot.

Try a few angles

Don't just take one shot from where you're standing. Try it from straight above. Try it from the side at table height. Some food looks best from the top, like pizza or a spread of small plates. Some looks best from the side, like a burger or a tall drink.

Don't use the flash

Phone flash on food almost never works. It flattens everything and makes shiny spots on sauces. Turn it off and find better light instead.

Shoot it while it's hot

Food changes fast. Sauces skin over, greens wilt, ice melts, cheese sets. Take the photo the moment the plate lands, then eat it.

The mix that works for most places

Most restaurants I talk to end up with a mix. Professional photos for the menu, the website, the apps and press. Phone photos for daily posts. Then a short dish drop or a monthly visit when new things come out, so the good photos stay current.
